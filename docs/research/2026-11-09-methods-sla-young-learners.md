# Second-language acquisition (SLA) research for a home Spanish app (children 6–10, 10–15 min/day)

> Research report produced on 2026-11-09 for the learning-method review (Second-language acquisition frameworks for young learners (and the parent's role)). Sources were restricted to peer-reviewed papers, meta-analyses, books by researchers and university/government sources; studies of specific commercial apps were excluded. Some full texts were unreachable from the research environment, so several figures come from abstracts or secondary summaries; confidence ratings reflect that. Synthesis: [`docs/learning-methodology.md`](../learning-methodology.md).

## Frameworks

**Input hypothesis / Natural Approach (Krashen).** The claim is that children acquire language by understanding messages pitched slightly above their level, in a low-anxiety setting, and that speaking emerges later. Evidence is **medium** that comprehensible input is central and **low** that it is enough on its own. Critics found the strong claims hard to test. Lichtman & VanPatten argue the core ideas survive under new names: implicit learning, a fixed order of development, and input embedded in communication. Sources: Krashen 1982, *Principles and Practice in SLA*; Krashen & Terrell 1983; Lichtman & VanPatten 2021, *Foreign Language Annals* 54(2):283–305, doi:10.1111/flan.12552.

**Output hypothesis (Swain).** Producing language makes learners notice the gaps in what they know. Swain's evidence was Canadian French-immersion children: after years of input they understood well but spoke inaccurately. Evidence **medium**: the origin is observational and causal tests are weaker. Source: Swain 1985, in Gass & Madden (eds.), *Input in SLA*.

**Interaction hypothesis (Long).** Negotiating meaning and getting feedback in conversation make input understandable and draw attention to form. Evidence **high** that interaction helps both vocabulary and grammar (Mackey & Goo 2007, meta-analysis in Mackey (ed.), *Conversational Interaction in SLA*, OUP). Children aged 8–13 negotiate meaning productively, and results from adults do not carry over automatically (Oliver 1998, *Modern Language Journal* 82(3):372–386). A screen offers only simulated contingency.

**Nation's four strands.** A course should give roughly equal time to four things: meaning-focused input, meaning-focused output, language-focused learning and fluency development. Evidence **medium**: it is a synthesis framework that has not been tested as a package. It works best as a design checklist. Source: Nation 2007, *Innovation in Language Learning and Teaching* 1(1):2–13, doi:10.2167/illt039.0.

**Task-Based Language Teaching (TBLT) for young learners.** Evidence **medium**. A meta-analysis of programmes found d = 0.93 (Bryfonski & McKay 2019, *Language Teaching Research* 23(5):603–632, doi:10.1177/1362168817744389). A re-analysis put it lower, at g ≈ 0.61. The most relevant single study is Shintani 2012 (*Language Teaching Research* 16(2):253–279). Six-year-old Japanese beginners did "listen-and-do" tasks, repeated 9 times over 5 weeks. They gained in recognising and producing the vocabulary, and in recognising plural -s, and they beat a control group. This directly supports listen-and-tap activities.

**Total Physical Response (TPR, Asher) and TPRS (storytelling).**
- **TPR:** Asher's own studies give **medium-low** evidence (Asher 1969, *Modern Language Journal* 53:3–17). Asher & Price 1967 (*Child Development*) reportedly found that adults outperformed children in the short term; I have not verified this.
- **Gesture/enactment research is stronger (medium-high):**
  - Five-year-olds who reproduced gestures recalled more L2 words, especially when they had to produce them (Tellier 2008, *Gesture* 8(2):219–235, doi:10.1075/gest.8.2.06tel).
  - Eight-year-olds who learned with gestures or with pictures did better than those who only listened, and the gains held 6 months later (Andrä et al. 2020, *Educational Psychology Review* 32:815–850, doi:10.1007/s10648-020-09527-z).
- **TPRS:** evidence **low**. Studies are mostly small, in high schools, and written by advocates.
- **Stories themselves:** 7–8-year-olds learned words from hearing the same stories repeatedly, and short explanations increased the gains (Elley 1989, *Reading Research Quarterly* 24(2):174–187).

**Formulaic chunks / Lexical Approach.** Learners store multiword phrases. These phrases let beginners communicate early and are later broken down into productive grammar. Evidence **medium**. Sources:
- Wray 2002, *Formulaic Language and the Lexicon*.
- Myles, Hooper & Mitchell 1998, *Language Learning* 48(3):323–363: classroom learners "unpacked" memorised chunks into rules.
- Boers & Lindstromberg 2012, *Annual Review of Applied Linguistics* 32:83–110, doi:10.1017/S0267190512000050.
- Myles & Mitchell's research project compared children starting French at ages 5, 7 and 11 (38 hours each). Younger children leaned heavily on chunks. The 11-year-olds advanced faster in grammar, but recognition vocabulary was similar across ages.

**Meta-analyses on explicit vs implicit instruction**
- Instruction beats exposure alone, explicit beats implicit, and the effects last (Norris & Ortega 2000, *Language Learning* 50(3):417–528).
- Explicit beats implicit for both simple and complex grammar features, and the advantage held on delayed tests (Spada & Tomita 2010, *Language Learning* 60(2):263–308, doi:10.1111/j.1467-9922.2010.00562.x). Goo et al. 2015 updated these results and confirmed them.
- Caveat: most participants were adolescents or adults. Lichtman 2016 (*Journal of Child Language*) found that 5–7-year-olds also benefited from brief explicit instruction.

**Meta-analyses on corrective feedback**
- Lyster & Saito 2010 (*Studies in SLA* 32:265–302, doi:10.1017/S0272263109990520) pooled 15 classroom studies. **Prompts** (getting the learner to self-correct) worked better than **recasts** (simply restating the correct form), and **younger learners benefited more**.
- Li 2010 (*Language Learning* 60(2):309–365, doi:10.1111/j.1467-9922.2010.00561.x) found a medium, lasting effect. Implicit feedback held up better over time.

## What the evidence says for 6–10-year-olds with ~10–15 min/day

**Age.** Hartshorne, Tenenbaum & Pinker 2018 (*Cognition* 177:263–277, doi:10.1016/j.cognition.2018.04.007) found grammar-learning ability stays high until about 17.4 years. Native-like grammar mostly requires starting before about age 10–12, mainly through immersion.

In school-like, low-input settings, older learners are faster:
- In the Barcelona Age Factor project, starters aged 11+ outpaced 8-year-olds given the same hours (Muñoz (ed.) 2006, *Age and the Rate of Foreign Language Learning*).
- The amount and quality of input predicted speaking better than starting age (Muñoz 2014, *Applied Linguistics* 35(4):463–482).
- Learning Dutch naturalistically, 3–5-year-olds were slowest and teenagers and adults fastest at first (Snow & Hoefnagel-Höhle 1978, *Child Development* 49:1114–1128; Snow later worked at Harvard Graduate School of Education).
- Among more than 5,000 German students, early starters' advantage at Grade 5 was overtaken by Grade 7 (Jaekel et al. 2017, *Language Learning* 67(3):631–664, doi:10.1111/lang.12242).
- English primary pupils doing French made "small but significant" progress. Teaching time mattered, and first-language literacy explained 30–40% of the variation (Graham et al. 2017, *Language Learning* 67(4); Courtney et al. 2017, *Applied Linguistics* 38(6)).

**Realistic goals.** 10–15 minutes a day comes to roughly 45–90 hours a year, comparable to a decent primary-school programme. That supports CEFR Pre-A1 to A1 young-learner level (Council of Europe 2018, descriptors for ages 7–10). In practice that means:
- strong listening on familiar topics;
- a few hundred high-frequency words plus useful chunks (this number is my estimate);
- good sound perception;
- a positive attitude.

It does **not** support accurate productive grammar. The pronunciation advantage of early starts is well documented in immersion; it is unproven at low input.

**Biggest levers**
1. A large amount of comprehensible, repeated input.
2. Repetition across days. The meta-analytic link between number of encounters and words learned is r = .34, and it is weaker for children (Uchihara, Webb & Yanagisawa 2019, *Language Learning* 69(3):559–599, doi:10.1111/lang.12343).
3. Distributed practice. Distributed lessons beat clustered ones for children (Seabrook, Brown & Solity 2005, *Applied Cognitive Psychology* 19(1):107–122).
4. A live social partner.
5. Motivation.
6. Brief explicit "noticing" for the 9–10-year-olds, who use analytic strategies more.

## The parent's role

**The evidence base.** Infants learned Mandarin sounds from live speakers but not from the same material on video or audio (Kuhl, Tsao & Liu 2003, *PNAS* 100(15):9096–9101, doi:10.1073/pnas.1532872100). Toddlers learned verbs from live interaction or contingent video chat, but not from pre-recorded video (Roseberry, Hirsh-Pasek & Golinkoff 2014, *Child Development* 85(3):956–970, doi:10.1111/cdev.12166). Infants learned more from video when a peer was present (Lytle, Garcia-Sierra & Kuhl 2018, *PNAS* 115(40):9859–9866, doi:10.1073/pnas.1611621115).

These studies are of infants and toddlers. School-age children do learn from media: in the ELLiE study of over 1,400 European children, TV exposure was linked to foreign-language listening (Lindgren & Muñoz 2013, *International Journal of Multilingualism* 10(1)). So the app can carry the input, and the parent adds the contingency.

**Harvard sources**
- Center on the Developing Child, "serve and return": notice, respond, name it, take turns and wait, practise endings.
- Among 4–6-year-olds, the number of conversational turns predicted language-related brain function, and the number of words heard did not (Romeo et al. 2018, *Psychological Science* 29(5):700–710, doi:10.1177/0956797617742725; Harvard/MIT, co-author Rowe).
- Rowe & Snow 2020 (*Journal of Child Language* 47(1):5–21) describe three dimensions of input quality: interactive, linguistic and conceptual.
- Brief guidance to parents increased back-and-forth conversation at home (Leech & Rowe 2021, *Journal of Child Language* 48(2):399–412).

**Guidance on joint use.** AAP 2016, "Media and Young Minds," *Pediatrics* 138(5):e20162591, doi:10.1542/peds.2016-2591. Takeuchi & Stevens 2011, *The New Coviewing* (Joan Ganz Cooney Center), notes that siblings count as co-users.

**A 5-minute routine; the parent does not need to speak Spanish**
1. **"Teach me" (2 min):** the child teaches the parent today's words and gestures. This gives the child output, retrieval practice and real turn-taking.
2. **TPR swap (1 min):** the app says a command, the child acts it out, then the child gives the command to the parent or a sibling.
3. **Chunk of the week:** use 2–3 phrases during daily routines (¿Tienes hambre? / ¡Buenos días!) and wait for the child's reply.
4. **Prompt, don't lecture:** when the child makes a mistake, ask "¿Cómo se dice…?" or replay the app's model, and praise the attempt.
5. **Story talk:** point at the pictures and ask "¿Qué es?" or "¿Dónde está…?". Link the story to the child's life ("¿Tienes un perro?").

## Design implications

1. **Listen before speaking.** Each new set of words starts with listen-and-tap tasks, repeated across several days, before the child is asked to produce anything (Shintani 2012; Nation 2007).
2. **Picture plus a gesture the child performs.** Ask the child to do the gesture while saying the word (Tellier 2008; Andrä et al. 2020).
3. **Teach chunks with slots** ("Quiero __", "Me gusta __", "¿Dónde está __?"). Later, show the slot that can change (Myles et al. 1998; Wray 2002).
4. **Plan many encounters per item across stories, TPR and review.** There is no proven magic number (Uchihara et al. 2019).
5. **Keep sessions short and daily, with spaced retrieval.** Don't over-engineer the review intervals. With 8–11-year-olds using a learning game, 3.5-day vs 7-day spacing made little difference, while individual differences mattered more (Kasprowicz, Marsden & Sephton 2019, *Modern Language Journal* 103(3):580–606, doi:10.1111/modl.12586; Seabrook et al. 2005).
6. **Balance the four strands each week.** For example: about 4 minutes of story or listening, 3 of TPR or speaking, 3 of review and noticing, and 2 of fast replay of familiar material (Nation 2007).
7. **Give optional 10–15-second explicit tips in English for ages 8–10.** Cover one feature at a time (el/la, plural -s), then follow with lots of input containing it (Spada & Tomita 2010; Lichtman 2016; Muñoz 2006).
8. **Use prompts before models for feedback.** Offer "try again" with a hint, then give the model (Lyster & Saito 2010). Child speech recognition is unreliable: error rates are around 40% on children's read speech versus under 2% for adults, and worst for the youngest children. So:
   - score leniently, for example only whether the key word was recognised;
   - never penalise the child;
   - let the parent override the result.
   An ASR-based pronunciation trainer matched teacher-led training in the short term for 11-year-olds (Neri et al. 2008, *Computer Assisted Language Learning* 21(5):393–408).
9. **Picture stories should be short, repetitive and re-heard on 3+ days**, with quick picture glosses for new words (Elley 1989).
10. **Use Spanish–English cognates as early wins** (animal, elefante, chocolate) and teach children to spot them (Carlo, August, McLaughlin, Snow et al. 2004, *Reading Research Quarterly* 39(2):188–215, doi:10.1598/rrq.39.2.3).
11. **Put an on-screen "grown-up turn" cue in every session** (Kuhl 2003; Roseberry 2014; serve and return).
12. **Make role-plays two-sided and swappable.** The child plays both roles and swaps with the parent. Offer a choice of replies so the exchange feels contingent (Long; Oliver 1998; Swain 1985).
13. **Give the child "I can…" goals** mapped to CEFR Pre-A1/A1 young-learner descriptors and to ACTFL/NCSSFL Novice Can-Do statements. Aim for 90%+ Spanish, supported by visuals and gestures, as ACTFL's 2010 target-language statement recommends. Keep English for brief tips and instructions to the parent.

## Open questions / could not verify

- **Full texts:** cambridge.org, PMC, UCL and McGill were unreachable from the research environment, so effect sizes and moderators come from abstracts and summaries.
- **Hartshorne et al.:** the "start by 10–12 for native-like" figure comes via press coverage. How their non-immersion learners fared is unverified. The finding applies to immersion, not to 15 minutes a day.
- **Asher & Price 1967:** the claim that adults beat children is from memory and not verified.
- **Early start and pronunciation:** not established for low-input settings.
- **Social contingency:** the evidence is from infants and toddlers. How much it matters for 6–10-year-olds learning from a screen is untested.
- **Speech recognition feedback:** no rigorous study covers English-speaking 6–10-year-olds learning Spanish. Neri et al. 2008 covers 11-year-olds, short-term only.
- **TPRS:** the evidence is mostly from high schools and written by advocates.
- **Harvard:** I found no Harvard study of English-speaking children learning a foreign language at home. The relevant Harvard work covers parent talk, vocabulary, cognates and the critical period, and applies by analogy:
  - Snow, Uccelli and Lesaux's academic-language work targets older students and English learners.
  - I found no direct Lesaux source.
- **Optimal repetition counts and review intervals for children:** unresolved.
- **ACTFL statements:** I saw the text only through secondary copies, and I could not confirm whether they have been revised since 2010/2012.
