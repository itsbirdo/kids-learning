# The "Army Method" for language learning: what it was, what the evidence shows, and what fits 6–10-year-olds

> Research report produced on 2026-11-09 for the learning-method review (Military and government language-training methods (the WWII "Army Method", ALM, FSI, DLI)). Sources were restricted to peer-reviewed papers, meta-analyses, books by researchers and university/government sources; studies of specific commercial apps were excluded. Some full texts were unreachable from the research environment, so several figures come from abstracts or secondary summaries; confidence ratings reflect that. Synthesis: [`docs/learning-methodology.md`](../learning-methodology.md).

**Access caveat:** the research environment's network settings blocked full-text fetches (files.eric.ed.gov, jbe-platform.com, rockefellerfoundation.org and similar hosts fail to resolve). Every finding below comes from search-indexed abstracts, catalogue records and secondary summaries. I mark confidence with that in mind.

## What the Army Method actually was

- **1941: the civilian forerunner.** The American Council of Learned Societies (ACLS) set up the Intensive Language Program. Mortimer Graves ran it on a Rockefeller Foundation grant, and J. Milton Cowan, Secretary-Treasurer of the Linguistic Society of America (LSA), directed it. By summer 1942 there were about 56 courses in 26 languages at 18 universities, with about 700 students (Graves & Cowan, *Report of the First Year's Operation of the Intensive Language Program of the ACLS, 1941–42*).
- **1942: the method itself.** The LSA published Leonard Bloomfield's 16-page *Outline Guide for the Practical Study of Foreign Languages*. It describes the "informant method": a native speaker supplies the model sentences, and a trained linguist analyses them and directs the drilling.
- **1943–44: the Army Specialized Training Program (ASTP).** Language courses began in April 1943. There were about 500 intensive speaking courses in more than 30 languages at 55 universities (Velleman 2008). Trainees were selected: enlisted men under 22 with an Army General Classification Test (AGCT) score of at least 110, later 115 (Keefer 1988). The programme was sharply cut in February 1944 because the Army needed combat troops.
- **What a week looked like.** About 15 contact hours a week. There was a daily drill hour, six days a week, with a native-speaker "drillmaster" and groups of 10 or fewer. A senior instructor (the linguist) added about 2 hours a week of structural analysis (Agard et al. 1944 survey, from search excerpts). Richards & Rodgers report 15 hours of drill plus 20–30 hours of private study, over two or three six-week sessions. Some accounts say up to 10 hours a day. The figures differ between sources.
- **Techniques.** Mimicry and memorisation of short everyday dialogues, pattern drills and recordings. These later became the Audio-Lingual Method (ALM) and the college language lab.
- **Myth versus fact.** Richards & Rodgers say the ASTP was new mainly in "the procedures used and the intensity of teaching rather than… its underlying theory." Moulton (1961) records "exaggerated, even fantastic claims" made for the "Army method." The success stories come from small classes of selected, mature, highly motivated young men with very many contact hours. They come from no controlled comparison. The idea that the method was built on Skinner's behaviourism is also wrong: the ASTP came before Skinner's *Verbal Behavior* (1957), and Castagnaro (2006) shows the link was overstated.

## Evidence

**The wartime programme**

- **Agard, Clements et al. (1944), *A Survey of Language Classes in the Army Specialized Training Program*.** MLA Commission on Trends in Education (ERIC ED031102). Site visits to 44 institutions. It is descriptive, with no outcome data. Its recommendations include applying the approach to elementary-school (FLES) and secondary programmes. **Confidence: medium** on structure; it is not evidence about effects.
- **Agard & Dunkel (1948), *An Investigation of Second-Language Teaching*.** Ginn (ERIC ED041490). It compared "aural-oral" experimental classes in schools and colleges with conventional classes. Results were inconclusive. Aural-oral students did not become "spontaneously fluent" in the time available, and their reading was weaker. Carroll (1954, *Notes on the Measurement of Achievement in Foreign Languages*) notes the main tests were print-based, which may have disadvantaged the oral groups. **Confidence: medium** (secondary summaries).
- **Contemporary accounts.** Angiolillo (1947), *Armed Forces' Foreign Language Teaching: Critical Evaluation and Implications* (Vanni); Matthew (1947), American Council on Education; Kaulfers (1944), "Wartime Developments in Modern-Language Achievement Testing," *Modern Language Journal* 28:136–150; Velleman (2008), *Historiographia Linguistica* 35(3):385–416, doi:10.1075/hl.35.3.05vel. Speaking was assessed with crude rating scales. ASTP outcomes are essentially **anecdotal**: **low confidence** in any "fluent in months" claim.

**The Audio-Lingual Method and its critics**

- **Scherer & Wertheimer (1964), *A Psycholinguistic Experiment in Foreign-Language Teaching*.** McGraw-Hill (ERIC ED015446). About 300 German students at the University of Colorado, 1960–62. ALM students were far ahead in speaking. Traditional students were slightly better at writing and translation. There was no difference in listening or reading, and ALM students had better attitudes. **Confidence: high** (abstract).
- **Smith (1970), *A Comparison of the Cognitive and Audiolingual Approaches* (the Pennsylvania Project).** Center for Curriculum Development. Pennsylvania high-school French and German, 1965–69. ALM ("functional skills") was no better than traditional teaching, traditional was equal or better in reading, and the type of language lab made no difference. Carroll (1969, *Foreign Language Annals*, doi:10.1111/j.1944-9720.1969.tb01281.x) found the "traditional" condition was a modified one and noted statistical flaws, but judged the main findings valid. **Confidence: medium-high** for "no ALM advantage"; **low** for "ALM was worse," which Wikipedia overstates.
- **Theoretical critiques.** Chomsky (1959), review of Skinner, *Language* 35(1):26–58. Rivers (1964), *The Psychologist and the Foreign-Language Teacher* (University of Chicago Press): drill without personal meaning, and ignoring emotion, are weaknesses. These are arguments, not data.
- **The drill debate.** Wong & VanPatten (2003), "The Evidence is IN: Drills are OUT," *Foreign Language Annals* 36(3):403–423: mechanical drills are not shown to be necessary. Leaver, Rifkin & Shekhtman (2004), *FLA* 37:125–132, practitioners from government programmes, dispute this. Jackson & Kaplan (2001), "Lessons learned from fifty years of theory and practice in government language teaching," Georgetown University Round Table 1999, pp. 71–87, report the Foreign Service Institute (FSI) experience that focused practice, "including drills," seems necessary for automatic use. **Confidence: low-medium**; the question is contested.
- **Practice is skill-specific.** DeKeyser (1997), *Studies in Second Language Acquisition* 19(2):195–221, doi:10.1017/S0272263197002040. Comprehension practice and production practice each automatise their own skill, gradually, along a power-law curve. **Confidence: high** (adults, artificial language).
- **Memorised chunks are a launch pad.** Myles, Hooper & Mitchell (1998), "Rote or rule?", *Language Learning* 48(3):323–363, doi:10.1111/0023-8333.00045. English schoolchildren learning French later "unpack" rote-learned chunks to build new sentences. Boers et al. (2006), *Language Teaching Research* 10(3):245–261, doi:10.1191/1362168806lr195oa: formulaic sequences raise perceived oral proficiency (n=32 adults). **Confidence: medium-high / medium.**

**Modern government training**

- **FSI.** Spanish is a Category I language: 24–30 weeks. The current State Department page says 552–690 class hours (older pages say 600–750), at about 23 class hours plus 17 self-study hours a week, to reach S-3/R-3 on the Interagency Language Roundtable (ILR) scale. The ILR scale runs from 0 to 5; level 3 is professional working proficiency. Teachers are native speakers, and groups are at most about 6 for Spanish (state.gov; govtilr.org). Jackson & Kaplan list aptitude, time on task and intensity, prior knowledge, and a supportive environment as key factors. **Confidence: high** for the hours; **medium** for the lessons (practitioner synthesis).
- **Defense Language Institute (DLIFLC).** Courses run 36 weeks for Category I/II up to 64 weeks for Category IV, with 6–7 class hours a day. The Proficiency Enhancement Program cut the student–teacher ratio from 10:2 to 6:2. Teaching is task-based and student-centred, with 1–3-day off-site "iso-immersion" events, including for Spanish (dliflc.edu). **Confidence: medium.** The Spanish course length conflicts: 26 weeks (AUSA) versus 36.
- **Aptitude.** The Defense Language Aptitude Battery (DLAB) predicts graduation consistently but only moderately, and predicts oral proficiency weakly (Naval Postgraduate School and DTIC studies). The Modern Language Aptitude Test (MLAT) correlates about .40–.65 with outcomes in intensive courses (Skehan 1998; Ehrman & Oxford 1995, *MLJ* 79(1):67–89). Carroll (1967), *FLA* 1(2):131–151 (2,782 college seniors): time abroad, an early start and home use all predicted proficiency. **Confidence: medium.**

**Children**

- **Total Physical Response (TPR), funded by the Office of Naval Research.** Asher (1968), DTIC AD0674868: across more than 21 experiments, acting out Russian commands produced better listening comprehension than translating them. Asher & Price (1967), *Child Development* 38:1219–1227: adults outperformed children in the short term. **Confidence: medium-high.**
- **Age and rate.** Muñoz (2006), *Age and the Rate of Foreign Language Learning* (Multilingual Matters), and Muñoz (2008), *Applied Linguistics* 29(4):578–596. In low-exposure classrooms, older starters progress faster, and younger starters only pull ahead after many more hours. **Confidence: high.**
- **Early-start programmes.** Burstall et al. (1974), *Primary French in the Balance* (NFER): UK pupils started French at 8 with audio-visual courses. The main lasting gain was attitude, not mastery. Disputed, but influential. The 1960s US FLES boom collapsed partly because of too few contact hours, unrealistic goals and poor continuity between grades (ERIC ED038064; Curtain & Dahlberg, *Languages and Learners: Making the Match*). **Confidence: medium.**
- **Self-access listening.** Lightbown, Halter, White & Horst (2002), "Comprehension-based learning: the limits of 'do it yourself'," *Canadian Modern Language Review* 58(3):427–464. Canadian schoolchildren learned English for 6 years by listening to recordings while reading, with no teacher instruction. They matched comparison pupils on comprehension and oral production, but had gaps in written accuracy. This is very relevant to an audio-first app. **Confidence: high.**
- **How time is spread.** Collins, Halter, Lightbown & Spada (1999), *TESOL Quarterly* 33(4):655–680 (Quebec children), and Serrano & Muñoz (2007), *System* 35:305–321 (adults): concentrated time beat thinly spread time in classroom programmes. Goossens et al. (2012), *Journal of Cognitive Psychology* 24(8):965–971: spaced review helped primary pupils' vocabulary. Their 2016 classroom follow-up was weaker. **Confidence: medium.**
- **ELLiE.** Enever (2011), *Early Language Learning in Europe* (British Council; about 1,200 children in 7 countries). Even limited exposure produces steady growth, but speaking develops slowly. Motivation starts high and may decline. Contact outside school helps. **Confidence: medium-high.**

## What carries over to a 6–10-year-old in a self-paced app

- **Native-speaker model audio first.** This is the "informant." An app can supply it without limit.
- **Short dialogues memorised by mimicry, built from reusable chunks.** Children store chunks and break them down later (Myles et al. 1998).
- **Listen and respond physically before speaking.** Tapping or acting on commands works (Asher), and comprehension-led learning alone gets surprisingly far for children (Lightbown et al. 2002).
- **One-to-one, immediate turns.** This resembles the small-group drill hour, and the app gives every child every turn.
- **A separate speaking step.** Practice is skill-specific (DeKeyser 1997), so tapping alone won't build speech.
- **Daily regularity and cumulative hours.** Every government programme stresses time on task.

## What doesn't carry over

- **Intensity.** 10–15 minutes a day is about 60–90 hours a year. FSI needs about 600 class hours for adults to reach professional level in Spanish. The 1960s FLES programmes failed partly on too few hours. Expect only basic comprehension and set phrases.
- **Selection and motivation.** ASTP and DLI trainees were aptitude-screened adults with career or wartime stakes. Children need intrinsic motivation, and the main proven gain from an early start is attitude (Burstall 1974).
- **Long mechanical drills.** Adults put up with hours of them; the evidence for them is contested even for adults (Wong & VanPatten 2003), and young children tend to disengage.
- **The linguist's grammar sessions.** Explicit analysis relies on cognitive maturity (Muñoz 2006). It is largely wasted on 6–8-year-olds and only marginal at 9–10.
- **Off-site immersion events.** These cannot be replicated in an app.

## Design implications

1. **Make every unit a 4–8-line, two-voice dialogue recorded by native speakers,** at natural and slowed speed, and reuse its lines as chunks in later units. Basis: Bloomfield's informant method; the FSI *Spanish Basic Course* (1961; Stockwell, Bowen, Silva-Fuenzalida), which is built around "basic sentences"; Myles et al. 1998.
2. **Listen and do before you say.** Each new chunk first appears in picture taps or "touch the…" commands, and spoken production comes only after the child has got these right. Basis: Asher 1968; Lightbown et al. 2002.
3. **Teach long lines with backward build-up:** "...la escuela" → "a la escuela" → "Voy a la escuela." Use the app's voice as a "say it with me" choral partner. Basis: Larsen-Freeman & Anderson, *Techniques and Principles in Language Teaching* (OUP 2011), audio-lingual chapter; DeKeyser 1997 on production practice.
4. **Use only meaningful drills.** The child's answer must depend on understanding something, such as the picture that changes, never on a mechanical substitution alone. Keep each drill burst to about 2 minutes; that limit is a heuristic, not tested. Basis: Paulston 1970, *FLA* 4(2):187–193; Rivers 1964; Wong & VanPatten 2003.
5. **Turn dialogues into role-play in stages:** child repeats → child takes one role while the app voices the other → roles swap → a variation in which a picture changes one slot. This mirrors the FSI course's variation drills and "conversation stimulus" section.
6. **Schedule spaced review of whole dialogues and chunks:** same session, next day, then roughly 3 days, 1 week and 1 month. Basis: Pimsleur 1967, "A Memory Schedule," *MLJ* 51(2):73–75, doi:10.1111/j.1540-4781.1967.tb06700.x; Goossens et al. 2012. Do not over-engineer the intervals, because the classroom evidence is mixed.
7. **Keep the 10–15-minute daily core, and add more exposure without extra work:** an optional audio-only "car mode" that replays the week's dialogues and songs. Basis: Jackson & Kaplan 2001 on time on task; Collins et al. 1999 on concentrated time; ELLiE on exposure outside school.
8. **Make speech-recognition feedback forgiving and encouraging,** a "you said it!" gate rather than a pronunciation score. Attitude is the most reliable early-start gain, and motivation tends to fall over time. Basis: Burstall 1974; Enever 2011; Scherer & Wertheimer 1964 on attitudes.
9. **No grammar explanations for 6–8-year-olds.** For 9–10-year-olds, add an optional one-line "notice the pattern" tip only after they have used the chunk. Basis: Muñoz 2006; Myles et al. 1998.
10. **Measure progress in understanding and small spoken tasks,** not "fluency." Show cumulative minutes to the parent, and give the parent a partner role in role-plays. Agard & Dunkel found no spontaneous fluency within the time given, and unrealistic goals helped sink FLES.

## Open questions and what I could not verify

- **Full texts not read:** Agard & Dunkel 1948, the 1944 survey, Velleman 2008, Angiolillo 1947, Matthew 1947 and Jackson & Kaplan 2001. The network allowlist blocked them.
- **ASTP hours:** weekly contact hours and session structure differ between sources.
- **ASTP outcomes:** there was never a controlled evaluation, so success claims are anecdotal.
- **DLI Spanish course length:** 26 versus 36 weeks, unresolved. Pimsleur's exact intervals were not checked against the original paper.
- **Lightbown (1983):** I could not verify the frequently cited claim that drilled forms are later dropped, so I left it out.
- **No studies of drills for this age in apps:** I found no study testing audio-lingual drills with 6–10-year-olds in apps. Everything about transfer to this age group is inference.
- **Speech-recognition accuracy:** I did not check how well on-device speech recognition handles children's non-native Spanish. It is likely the biggest practical risk.
- **Spacing for children:** the evidence for spacing versus massing in children's classrooms is mixed (Goossens et al. 2016).
