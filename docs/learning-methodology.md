# Learning method: how the app teaches, and why

Date: 2026-11-09 · Status: proposed. It drives the lesson content (#5, #6) and delivery (#11, #12, #13, #14, #17, #21).

This is the synthesis of three research reviews in [`docs/research/`](research/):
- [military and government methods](research/2026-11-09-methods-army-and-government-training.md)
- [memory science](research/2026-11-09-methods-memory-science.md)
- [second-language acquisition (SLA) for young learners](research/2026-11-09-methods-sla-young-learners.md)

Sources were limited to peer-reviewed papers, meta-analyses, researchers' books and university or government sources. Studies of specific commercial apps were excluded.

## 1. The method in one picture
Every word and phrase goes through the same loop, spread over days:

```
HEAR it ──► DO it ──► SAY it ──► USE it ──► REVIEW it (spaced, after sleep)
picture +    tap / act   repeat, then   dialogue,     next day, +3, +7, +14,
gesture,     ("toca…")   recall from    story, chant, +30, +60, +120 days
model voice              a picture      parent turn
```

**Three ideas carry most of the weight:**
1. **Lots of repeated, understandable listening.** Hearing comes first, and doing (tapping, acting) comes before saying.
2. **Retrieval, spaced over days.** The child recalls the word rather than just seeing it again.
3. **Ready-made phrases ("chunks") in short dialogues,** learned by imitation from a native-speaker voice. This is the useful core of the WWII "Army Method".

Everything else is about keeping a 6–10-year-old motivated and the practice forgiving.

## 2. The "Army Method": what we take and what we leave
**What it was.** The WWII Army Specialized Training Program (ASTP, 1943–44) grew out of the American Council of Learned Societies' 1941 Intensive Language Program and Leonard Bloomfield's "informant method".
- Small groups (10 or fewer) drilled daily with a native-speaker "drillmaster".
- A linguist explained the structure.
- Learners memorised short everyday dialogues by imitation and did pattern drills.
- About 15 contact hours a week, plus private study.

**The evidence.** Its fame rests on anecdote. Trainees were aptitude-screened, motivated young adults with very many hours, and there was no controlled evaluation. Researchers at the time warned of "exaggerated, even fantastic claims" (Moulton 1961).

Its successor, the Audio-Lingual Method, had mixed results:
- It beat traditional teaching on speaking and attitude (Scherer & Wertheimer 1964).
- It showed no advantage in the Pennsylvania Project (Smith 1970).

Modern government schools now teach in small groups with native speakers, task-based. The Foreign Service Institute (FSI) budgets about 600 class hours for an adult to reach professional Spanish.

| We **take** | Why it fits a 6–10-year-old in an app |
|---|---|
| Native-speaker model audio for everything (the "informant") | An app can supply unlimited perfect models |
| A short two-voice **core dialogue** per unit, memorised by imitation | Children store chunks and later break them down into grammar (Myles, Hooper & Mitchell 1998) |
| **Backward build-up** for long lines ("…la escuela" → "a la escuela" → "Voy a la escuela") | A classic audio-lingual technique; makes production manageable |
| Varying one slot in a phrase **with meaning**: the picture changes, and the answer depends on it | The useful part of pattern drills, without mindless substitution (Paulston 1970; Wong & VanPatten 2003) |
| Every child gets every turn, immediately | The small-group drill hour, scaled down to one-to-one |
| Daily regularity; time on task counts | Every government programme stresses hours (Jackson & Kaplan 2001) |

| We **leave** | Why |
|---|---|
| Intensity (hours a day) | 10–15 min/day ≈ 60–90 h/year. Goals must be modest (§6) |
| Long mechanical drills | Contested even for adults; young children disengage |
| Grammar lectures | They depend on maturity (Muñoz 2006). At most, a one-line tip for ages 8–10 |
| Selection and high stakes | Children need intrinsic motivation. The proven early-start gain is attitude (Burstall 1974; Enever 2011) |

## 3. Teaching rules
Each rule links to the issue that implements it. Confidence ratings: **H** = strong evidence (e.g. meta-analyses), **M** = several studies or indirect evidence, **L** = small or advocate-led studies.

### Content (#5, #6)
1. **Every unit is built around a core dialogue** of 4–8 lines and two voices, plus **chunks with slots** ("Quiero __", "Me gusta __", "¿Dónde está __?"). Later units reuse earlier chunks. *(M: Myles et al. 1998; Wray 2002; Boers & Lindstromberg 2012; FSI Spanish Basic Course "basic sentences".)*
2. **Picture + a simple gesture the child copies** for every word. Watching the gesture still helps a child who won't move. *(M-H: Andrä et al. 2020, 8-year-olds, gains held 6 months; Tellier 2008, 5-year-olds; Macedonia & Knösche 2011.)*
3. **New-word sets mix categories.** Don't introduce six colours or six animals together; draw sets from a scene or story. *(M: Erten & Tekin 2008, 9–10-year-olds; Nakata & Suzuki 2019; Brunmair & Richter 2019: for word pairs, blocking by category didn't help.)*
4. **Cognates as early wins** (animal, elefante, chocolate, tren), and teach the child to spot them. *(M: Carlo, August, McLaughlin, Snow et al. 2004; Snow is at Harvard Graduate School of Education.)*
5. **Short, repetitive picture stories** re-heard on at least 3 days, with picture glosses for new words. *(M: Elley 1989, 7–8-year-olds; Lightbown et al. 2002 showed listening-led learning goes far for children.)*
6. **Chants or songs with a fixed melody** for phrases. They add to retrieval practice; they don't replace it. *(L-M: Ludke et al. 2014; Good et al. 2015.)*
7. **Explicit tips only for ages 8–10:** one optional 10–15-second English line ("notice: *el* perro, *la* casa"), shown *after* the child has used the chunk. *(M-H: Spada & Tomita 2010; Lichtman 2016; Muñoz 2006.)*

### Delivery (#11, #12, #17)
8. **Listen before speaking.** A new item is heard and tapped or acted on, over several days if needed, before the child is asked to say it. *(M-H: Shintani 2012, 6-year-olds doing listen-and-do tasks; Asher 1969 on acting out commands; Zamuner et al. 2018 found that at 4.5–6, speaking brand-new words can even hurt recognition.)*
9. **Show before you test.** Play each new word 2–3 times with its picture and gesture before the first retrieval. Never make a beginner guess a word they haven't heard. *(M: Warmington, Hitch & Gathercole 2013, errorless learning better for 7–9-year-olds.)*
10. **Retrieve, don't re-watch.** Aim for at least 5 successful retrievals of each new word across today and tomorrow. *(H: Roediger & Karpicke 2006; Rowland 2014; Adesope et al. 2017; Karpicke, Blunt & Smith 2016 with primary-school children; Nakata 2017.)*
11. **Space within the session, and again after sleep.**
    - Re-quiz a new word after 2–3 other items, then again at the end of the session.
    - The first review is the next day. Don't judge mastery on the same day.
    *(H: Cepeda et al. 2006/2008; Karpicke & Roediger 2007; Henderson et al. 2012, 7–12-year-olds' word memory improves after sleep; Sobel, Cepeda & Kapler 2011, 5th graders ≈ 3× recall.)*
12. **Expanding review intervals, extended:** +1, +3, +7, +14, +30, +60, +120 days, with ±1 day of slack. Expanding vs fixed intervals hardly matters for final recall, but expanding keeps practice success high, which young learners need. *(H: Latimier et al. 2021; Kang et al. 2014; Cepeda et al. 2008; Küpper-Tetzel et al. 2014, 11–12-year-olds.)*
13. **Small batches, matched to age.**

    | Age | New items per session | Introduced in groups of |
    |---|---|---|
    | 6–7 | 3–4 | 2 |
    | 8–10 | 4–6 | 2–3 |

    A multi-word chunk counts as about 2 items. *(M: Riggs et al. 2006 and Cowan 2016 on working memory growth across ages 5–10.)*
14. **Practise speaking separately from listening.** Speaking only improves with speaking practice. Keep separate `listen` and `say` progress for each word. *(H for adults: DeKeyser 1997; M: Swain 1985; Webb 2009.)*
15. **Dialogues progress in stages:**
    1. listen
    2. repeat (with build-up)
    3. child takes one role while the app voices the other
    4. swap roles
    5. a variation where a picture changes one slot

    *(M: the FSI basic-course structure; Long's interaction hypothesis, Oliver 1998 with 8–13-year-olds.)*
16. **Feedback: hint first, then the model, then move on.**
    - After a miss, give a hint ("¿Cómo se dice…?" plus the picture). Then play the model and let the child retry and succeed.
    - Misses come back at the end of the session.
    - Never penalise.
    *(H: Lyster & Saito 2010, prompts beat recasts and younger learners benefit more; Metcalfe 2017; Metcalfe, Kornell & Finn 2009 on delayed feedback.)*
17. **Speech recognition is a forgiving gate, not a judge.**
    - A rejection from the recognizer counts as *uncertain*, not a miss, for ages 6–7.
    - A parent can override it.
    *(Children's recognition error rates are high; Neri et al. 2008 found speech-recognition-based training matched a teacher for 11-year-olds in the short term. See #16, #17, #19.)*
18. **Keep retrieval effortful but mostly successful.** Pause new words when review accuracy drops below about 75% or the backlog grows. The 75% figure is a heuristic, not a research result. *(M: Bjork & Bjork 2011; Fazio & Marsh 2019.)*
19. **Balance Nation's four strands within each session:**
    - meaning-focused input (story, dialogue)
    - meaning-focused output (say it, role-play)
    - language-focused learning (new words, tips)
    - fluency (fast replay of familiar items)

    *(M: Nation 2007, used as a design checklist.)*

### Motivation and the family (#13, #14)
20. **Rewards show progress; they don't pay for finishing.**
    - No "finish the lesson, get a sticker".
    - Use mastery displays ("words I can say"), occasional surprise stickers, and specific praise ("You remembered *perro* from last week!").
    - No streaks to lose.
    *(M-H: Deci, Koestner & Ryan 1999: expected, completion-contingent rewards reduce children's motivation (d ≈ −0.36); informational feedback helps. Praise-type effects are small: Li & Bates 2019; Sisk et al. 2018.)*
21. **A five-minute "grown-up turn" ends each session.** The app suggests one routine. The parent doesn't need to speak Spanish:
    - **"Teach me":** the child teaches today's words and gestures to the parent.
    - **TPR swap:** the child gives Spanish commands to the parent or a sibling.
    - **Chunk of the week:** 2–3 phrases used in daily routines ("¿Tienes hambre?"), waiting for the child's reply.
    - **Story talk:** point and ask "¿Qué es?", "¿Dónde está…?".
    - **Prompt, don't lecture:** when the child makes a mistake, ask "¿Cómo se dice…?" or replay the model.

    *(M: Kuhl, Tsao & Liu 2003; Roseberry, Hirsh-Pasek & Golinkoff 2014: live, responsive interaction beats recorded media for young children. Harvard Center on the Developing Child, "serve and return". Romeo et al. 2018, Harvard/MIT: back-and-forth conversational turns, not word counts, predicted language-related brain function. Leech & Rowe 2021: brief guidance raises parent–child back-and-forth. AAP 2016 on co-using media. The infant studies transfer only partly to ages 6–10.)*
22. **More listening without more lessons:** an optional parent-started "listen again" mode replays the week's dialogues, story and chants (car, bath, bedtime). *(M: time on task, Jackson & Kaplan 2001; Lindgren & Muñoz 2013, ELLiE study with 1,400+ children: media exposure is linked to listening.)*

## 4. The session (default 12 min)
| Step | Min | Strand | What happens |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1. Hello + warm-up | 1–2 | Fluency | Greeting routine; fast, easy replay of well-known chunks |
| 2. Review | 3 | Language-focused | Due cards as retrieval (tap, act, say), mixed across topics (rules 10–12) |
| 3. New | 3 | Language-focused | 2–3 new items at a time: show 2–3× with picture and gesture → tap retrieval → re-quiz after 2–3 items (rules 8–9, 11, 13) |
| 4. Use it | 3 | Meaning-focused input | Core-dialogue stage, or a story re-hear, or TPR (rules 1, 5, 15) |
| 5. Say it | 1–2 | Meaning-focused output | Production for items whose `listen` card is at box ≥ 2; hint-first feedback (rules 14, 16, 17) |
| 6. End check | 1 | Retrieval | Today's new items and misses once more (rules 11, 16) |
| 7. Wrap-up | 1 | — | Informational recap; "grown-up turn" card; "¡Hasta mañana!". Ends, with no autoplay (rules 20, 21) |

## 5. Ages 6–7 vs 8–10
| | Ages 6–7 | Ages 8–10 |
|---|---|---|
| New items per session | 3–4 | 4–6 |
| Speaking | Mostly repeat-after-me; picture → say only once the item is well known | Picture → say earlier; role-swaps |
| Explicit tips | None | Optional one-liners after use |
| Text | Pictures only; captions off | Optional captions; spotting familiar written words |
| Speech recognition | Rejection = uncertain; very lenient | Lenient; calibrated stars (#19) |
| Tone | Playful, mascot-led | "Not babyish" check in playtests |

## 6. Realistic goals
At 10–15 minutes a day (about 60–90 hours a year), the research supports these goals:
- **Level:** CEFR Pre-A1 to A1 young-learner level / ACTFL Novice Mid.
- **Listening:** strong on familiar topics.
- **Vocabulary:** a few hundred high-frequency words, plus chunks.
- **Sound:** good perception of Spanish sounds.
- **Attitude:** positive.

It does **not** support accurate productive grammar.

In low-input settings, older children progress faster than younger ones (Muñoz 2006, 2014; Jaekel et al. 2017). Harvard's Hartshorne, Tenenbaum & Pinker (2018) found the "start early for native-like grammar" advantage only with immersion. The 1960s US elementary-language programmes (FLES) failed partly on unrealistic goals.

So progress is measured with "I can" tasks and simple listening checks, never "fluency" (#14, #25).

## 7. What is still uncertain
- **Our exact setup is untested.** No study tests a spaced-review app with English-speaking 6–10-year-olds learning Spanish. Intervals come from adult studies and classroom studies of 8–12-year-olds.
- **Classroom effects can vanish.** Benefits disappeared when built into ordinary classroom exercises (Goossens et al. 2016). So we keep the rules strict and measure with our own kids.
- **Saying brand-new words aloud at age 6** may help or hurt. Hence "listen first", and production only for familiar items.
- **Social-contingency evidence** is mostly from infants and toddlers. The parent routine is cheap insurance, not a proven requirement.
- **Speech recognition on children's beginner Spanish** is the biggest practical risk. See the spike (#4) and calibration (#19).
